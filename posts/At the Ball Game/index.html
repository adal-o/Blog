At the Ball Game

I have never been a sports person. I have never paid the slightest attention to what games were taking place and who was winning or losing. Yet, when I went to my first-ever Dodgers game this past year, I was met with a somewhat absurd, but very real, wave of deep emotion and pride for my city.

In what I had always imagined to be a “boy’s club” rooted in White-Americana, I was confronted with baseball game that hosted a diverse crowd of old and young, women decked out in bedazzled Dodgers merchandise, nurses that were still in their scrubs having just rushed to the game from work, fans that flew out from Japan to witness the wonder of Shohei Ohtani, and of course, the archetype of the classic backwards-cap-wearing sports man I initially pictured. Aside from this crowd that challenged my pre-conceived notions, I also found a plethora of Korean and Japanese-inspired food options, language games in Spanish, and joyous pockets of people singing and dancing on self-made dance floors. Just as transformatively, I also took part in doing the wave and somehow knew every word to “Take Me Out to the Ball Game” despite not having gone to a ball game before.

Within these resonant observations, I found a missing piece that many of us subconsciously crave all the time: a singular, universal language that transcended all differences. There was an unexpected beauty in how the Dodgers game incorporated numerous cultures to facilitate a space that represented the depth of Los Angeles. It was an act of placemaking and community building I failed to acknowledge. Like the wave that was built with the individual efforts of over 40,000 people, I realized that the game, outside of baseball itself, was a champion of unity where people felt seen and heard. From that day on, the Dodgers gained one more fan that was able to share a single moment in time to celebrate something everyone loved, together.
